2. Evidence Calibration: Established Consensus vs. Open Questions

What Is Well Established

  • Broadly Comparable Pain & Strength: Both tenotomy and tenodesis deliver reliable pain relief, ASES scores, and biceps strength recovery.
  • Cosmetic Protection: Tenodesis substantially lowers the risk of Popeye deformity (~6.8% vs. ~23.3% in Level-I pooled trials; ~3.1–3.3× lower odds/risk).
  • Resource & Time Efficiency: Tenotomy eliminates implant expenditure and shortens operative time (~15 min pooled mean difference, though technique-dependent).
  • Biomechanical Construct Differences: Interference screws, cortical buttons, and all-suture anchors vary in initial stiffness, cyclic displacement, and cortical defect size.
  • Cortical Stress Risers: Larger subpectoral bone tunnels reduce torsional bone strength in biomechanical testing, creating a potential stress riser.
  • Location Equivalence on PROMs: Arthroscopic suprapectoral and mini-open subpectoral tenodesis generally yield similar patient-reported outcomes.

What Remains Uncertain

  • Clinical Superiority of Any Single Implant: Whether differences in time-zero stiffness or cyclic displacement translate into fewer clinical failures.
  • Subpectoral Pain Advantage: Whether distal subpectoral placement meaningfully reduces persistent anterior shoulder pain compared with suprapectoral fixation.
  • Optimal Patient Selection Thresholds: Exact age, BMI, or activity cutoffs defining which subgroup derives the clearest net benefit from tenodesis over tenotomy.
  • Fracture Risk Reduction via Smaller Tunnels: Whether smaller-footprint onlay or unicortical techniques translate into measurably lower clinical fracture rates given the already rare baseline incidence (~0.08% in large series).

3. Detailed Domain Synthesis

A. Tenotomy vs. Tenodesis: Clinical & Economic Selection

Across randomized evidence, pain, ASES scores, and elbow flexion/supination strength are generally similar between tenotomy and tenodesis, while tenodesis substantially reduces cosmetic Popeye deformity (~6.8% vs. ~23.3% in Level-I pooled trials) and may reduce bicipital cramping (OR ≈ 3.36 in pooled RCT analysis, though results vary across reviews). While some meta-analyses report statistically better Constant or SST scores with tenodesis, the absolute point differences appear small and may not be clinically meaningful.

Operative Time & Patient Selection: Tenotomy is typically faster; pooled evidence suggests approximately 15 minutes shorter operative time, although estimates vary substantially by technique and surgical setting (high statistical heterogeneity). Tenotomy may remain an attractive option in older, lower-demand patients or when minimizing operative time and implant use is prioritized, whereas tenodesis is often favored when preservation of arm contour is important, in younger or active patients, or based on explicit patient preference.

B. Fixation Constructs: All-Suture Anchors, Cortical Buttons & Interference Screws

Rather than following a rigid mechanical hierarchy, comparative biomechanical studies show conflicting load-to-failure rankings depending on testing setup:

  • Interference Screws (PEEK / Biocomposite): Frequently demonstrate high initial stiffness and strong fixation in biomechanical testing, but comparative studies do not establish a universally superior fixation construct.
  • All-Suture Anchors: Reported ultimate failure loads vary substantially with anchor design, number and configuration of sutures, tendon quality, and testing protocol—with some cadaveric models showing comparable or higher ultimate load than interference screws alongside greater cyclic displacement.
  • Cortical Buttons (Suspensory Fixation): Utilize a small drill hole with unicortical or bicortical suspensory button fixation (terminology varies by technique). Cadaveric studies demonstrate viable failure loads comparable to interference screws; bicortical drilling carries a technical/anatomic risk to posterior neurovascular structures if over-drilled, which unicortical anterior techniques aim to avoid.

Humeral Fracture Context: Large subpectoral bone tunnels can create a humeral stress riser; clinically apparent humeral fracture remains rare (~0.08% in large multi-thousand-case series) but potentially serious, and has been reported across multiple fixation methods (interference screws, cortical buttons, and suture anchors).

"Biomechanical superiority is construct- and technique-dependent, and has not consistently translated into clinically meaningful differences."

C. Onlay vs. Inlay Fixation

Inlay Fixation (Socket or Bone-Tunnel Docking): Docks the biceps tendon into a prepared bone socket (typically 6–8 mm diameter for interference screws) or smaller transosseous/all-suture bone tunnels. While inlay docking provides tendon-bone contact inside a prepared channel, biomechanical superiority over cortical onlay fixation is mixed and construct-dependent: a small matched-pair cadaveric study at the superior groove (Cagle et al., 2018; n = 6 pairs) reported comparable mean ultimate loads for interference-screw inlay (215 N; range 60–329 N) versus suture-anchor onlay (210 N), whereas a 2024 subpectoral cadaveric model reported higher failure load for an implantless all-suture inlay technique (588 N) compared with a unicortical metal-button onlay (376 N).

Onlay Fixation (Cortical Surface): Secures the tendon directly against prepared cortical bone using all-suture anchors or a cortical button. The theoretical rationale for cortical onlay fixation is minimizing cortical defect size (potentially reducing diaphyseal stress risers compared with larger subpectoral sockets) and facilitating direct visual length-tension adjustment, though these remain biomechanical/technical rationales rather than proven clinical differences; comparative clinical failure rates remain low across both strategies.

D. Anatomical Footprint Location: Intra-Articular, Top-of-Groove, Suprapectoral & Subpectoral

Intra-Articular / Soft-Tissue Tenodesis vs. Top-of-Groove Bone Fixation: Incorporating the biceps tendon directly into an anterior rotator cuff repair suture/anchor constitutes a soft-tissue tenodesis at the articular margin—saving operative time and an additional implant during concurrent cuff repair—whereas dedicated top-of-groove fixation secures the tendon into bone at the proximal entrance of the bicipital groove. Both leave tendon distal to the fixation point within the bicipital sheath.

Suprapectoral (In-Groove / Distal Groove) vs. Subpectoral Tenodesis: Comparative evidence generally demonstrates similar functional and pain outcomes (VAS, Constant, Popeye rates, and clinically equivalent ASES scores) between arthroscopic suprapectoral and mini-open subpectoral tenodesis. Subpectoral fixation places the tenodesis distal to the bicipital tunnel and may theoretically or clinically offer an advantage when residual groove pathology is a concern, though the magnitude and clinical importance of this difference remain uncertain. Conversely, the open subpectoral approach carries distinct procedural risks—including rare neurovascular injury (musculocutaneous or radial nerve), diaphyseal humeral fracture, and wound infection—that must be weighed against all-arthroscopic suprapectoral workflows.
